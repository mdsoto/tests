Clay minerals in 3D

Schematic of the five clay groups, after Brindley.

Clay minerals are layered aluminosilicates built from two kinds of sheets: tetrahedral (T) sheets of SiO₄ and octahedral (O) sheets of Al or Mg bonded to O and OH. A layer is either 1:1 (T + O) or 2:1 (T + O + T). The layer thickness, the charge, and what sits between layers (nothing, K⁺, hydrated cations, or a hydroxide sheet) set each mineral’s swelling and cation exchange capacity (CEC). Pick a mineral, then use the sheet buttons to highlight its tetrahedral or octahedral layers.

Si–Al and Al–Mg mark sites where one cation replaces another of similar size (isomorphous substitution), not compounds. In Si–Al (tan), Al³⁺ replaces Si⁴⁺ in a tetrahedral site. In Al–Mg (purple), Mg²⁺ replaces Al³⁺ in an octahedral site. Each substitution lowers the cation charge and leaves the layer with a permanent negative charge, which is balanced by exchangeable interlayer cations (K⁺, Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺). This is the origin of the CEC. Tetrahedral substitution (illite, mica, vermiculite) sits closer to the interlayer and binds cations more strongly than octahedral substitution (montmorillonite).

CEC is a consequence of the clay structure: the more cation substitution a layer has, the larger its negative charge and the more exchangeable cations it holds. Accessibility of the interlayer also matters: K⁺ is locked in illite and mica, while hydrated interlayers in montmorillonite and vermiculite are open.
